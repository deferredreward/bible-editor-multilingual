One verse — is it coherent?

“Interfaces should not contain information that is irrelevant or rarely needed. Every extra unit of information competes with the relevant units and diminishes their relative visibility.” — Nielsen Norman Group, Aesthetic and Minimalist Design

Two screens for the editor / exegete, not for a project manager: open one verse, see all of it, and judge whether it hangs together. Loaded with real Zechariah 1 — verses, original words, notes, word links, questions, and real translationAcademy and translationWords articles.

Verse — investigate

Original, literal and simplified, plus one detail area. Click any word in any text and the same place lights up in all three. Read for sense; Audit for a row-per-word comparison.

Focus — decide and move on

One column, top to bottom: a computed opening line, the three texts, then each note with what both translations actually did with its words. Arrow keys step verses.

The idea: anchor everything to the original word

Both legacy screens stack resources in boxes and leave the reader to correlate them by eye. But the literal and the simplified texts are each already aligned to the same original words. Joining them on that alignment — rather than merely showing them side by side — turns "is this coherent?" from a judgement you reconstruct into a lookup.

The clearest example is the first verse. A note calls הָיָה דְבַר־יְהוָה אֶל־זְכַרְיָה an idiom. Selecting it shows what each translation did with those exact words:

That is the exegete's question answered directly: the literal text keeps the idiom, the simplified text replaces it with a plain statement and moves the speaker to the front. Nothing had to be counted or cross-referenced by hand.

What was removed

Counting only what is permanently on screen, per resource, in the two screens these replace:

  • Per-panel toolbars. gatewayEdit gives every one of its six panels its own drag handle, minimise, save, link, hide and kebab. That is roughly thirty controls competing with the text. Here there are none.
  • Panel headers and tabs. No "translationNotes" bar above the notes — a heading and a count is enough.
  • Progress strips, done/all toggles, APPROVE ALL, NEW, translation-mode chips, per-row save and delete. All editing and workflow chrome: this is a reading and checking surface, so none of it belongs.
  • Resizable, rearrangeable regions. Manipulating the layout was the thing you called hard to manipulate. There is one control per screen and it names a task (Read / Audit), not a panel.

What is kept is what the task needs: the original with its morphology, both renderings, the notes with their real articles, the term links with theirs, and the questions.

Flags are observations, and stay quiet on purpose

A word the simplified text does not render is worth seeing — but an unrendered preposition or object marker is normal, and flagging it would be exactly the noise the heuristic warns against. So holes are split by real morphology: only a missing content word raises the amber line; function words get a quiet mention. Across Zechariah 1 that is of verses raising anything at all.

Nothing here is a verdict. The screens do not say a verse is wrong; they say where the three texts diverge, and leave the call to the person reading. A simplified text can carry a word's sense without a matching word.

One thing the data taught us

Also here: the first attempt

Ledger and Sweep were built first and answer a different question — where is the work across a whole book, and is the package internally consistent? They are project-manager instruments, not exegete ones, and are kept because that job is real too. Among other things they show that Zechariah's notes quote Hebrew in chapters 1–5 and 13 but English in chapters 6–12 and 14, and that 15 note quotes no longer match the literal text at all.

Status and limits

  • Mockups. No backend, nothing writes, nothing saves.
  • Zechariah 1 only — 21 verses, so the fixture stays a sensible size. The extractor takes a chapter constant.
  • Desktop only, deliberately. Both screens say so below 1100px instead of hiding something that matters.
  • Article prose is read from local en_ta / en_tw checkouts at build time. It is real unfoldingWord text, but it is a snapshot, not a live fetch.
  • Verified by driving the pages, not by eye — see the README for exactly what was and was not checked.
